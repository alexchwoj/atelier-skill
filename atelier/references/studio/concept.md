# Concept: finding the world

How to arrive at a look that belongs to one client and could not be guessed from the category. Used by the Studio route, and by any build where no library prompt or design fits. The Baseline and `foundations/anti-slop.md` still hold; nothing else is prescribed.

Aim higher than a good page: make the one a design lead would bring to a client who has already turned down every template.

## 1. Learn the subject

What only this client has: the name and what it means, the place and how it looks, what it does in one verb, its real numbers, its hours and rituals, what its world makes, prints or wears, the words its customers use. Then one line each:

- **Mechanism:** the one thing the product does that others do not.
- **Scene:** who meets it, where, under what light (this decides light or dark).
- **Cultural home:** the world the audience lives in outside this product.
- **What the first screen must prove.**

## 2. Name the rut

Write down the page this category always ships, and its predictable opposite. They are not banned; they are the bar. Every direction you consider must look at least as good as the category default executed well, and be more specific to this product.

- *Fintech:* navy, white cards, a phone mockup, a trust row. Opposite: neon on black with glowing charts.
- *Bakery:* cream paper, a serif, terracotta, a flour-dusted photo. Opposite: a stark black-and-white minimal grid.
- *AI tool:* dark, purple-blue gradient, glass cards, "the future of X". Opposite: brutalist mono with a blinking caret.

The four looks every model drifts toward:

1. Warm cream ground, high-contrast serif display, terracotta or signal-red accent.
2. Near-black with one neon accent and glowing edges.
3. Broadsheet hairlines, italic display serif, small tracked mono labels.
4. Flat saturated ink, black boxes, hard offset shadows, heavy condensed type.

Each is legitimate and can be beautiful. What fails is landing in one by reflex and stopping there: generic copy, stock decisions, nothing only this product has. If the honest best direction is one of these, take it and make it specific (real material, a detail from the product's world, a precise accent, a better-than-usual execution). Never trade it for a look that is merely different and worse.

## 3. Go wide: seven worlds

From the audience's own world, list seven concrete visual systems, artifacts, places or rituals they know by heart. For each, one line on why it resonates and can carry the mechanism, and one on how it looks (ground and main colours, type voices, the one signature that makes it recognisable).

- Draw from **at least three material families** (print, architecture, signage, textiles, instruments, broadcast, packaging, maps, sport, nature, food, transport, games…). More than three candidates from one family means the search stopped at the most obvious artifact.
- Include the audience's **graphic and screen traditions**, not only physical objects: the notation, publications, identity programmes, data graphics and interfaces they read daily. A nameable system (Swiss railway signage, a 1970s field guide, a race programme, a seed catalogue) is as concrete as any object.
- Ask what this thing would look like **as a physical object**, and what its world looked like **before the web**.
- On Sell and Show surfaces, the tools the audience operates (a terminal for developers, a mixing desk for musicians) count toward the rut: at most one candidate. On Work and Read surfaces, take inspiration from the audience's culture, never a costume of their instruments.

Example, *a booking app for climbing gyms*: route-setting tape and grade charts; topo guidebooks (hand-drawn lines on photos); chalk and rubber mats; competition bib numbers and heat sheets; trail blazes on rock; gym membership punch cards; weather and conditions boards at a crag.

## 4. Three directions, then one

Turn the best three worlds into complete directions. **Spread them** so the choice is real: one is the strongest library build made specific to this product; one commits harder to the world than feels comfortable; one is the wildcard, an unexpected world or structure that still sells. Each joins a reusable visual system to a concrete first screen:

```
WORLD        the source, in one line
LOOK         ground, ink, accent (with dosage), type voices, one material detail
FIRST SCREEN what is where and at what scale; where the action sits
SIGNATURE    the one moment a visitor would remember
RISK         the honest weakness (too familiar, hard to execute, needs photos we lack…)
```

Then sketch all three as rendered first screens and compare them on a board (`studio/explore.md` § 1); judge the pictures, not the paragraphs. Choose on three questions: **will it look excellent when built** (judge against the library prompts it resembles), **would the audience recognise themselves in it**, and **does it make the product clearer**. Novelty is not a criterion. Then **strengthen the winner with the runner-up's discipline**: name one quality the second direction had that the first lacks (a palette's total commitment, a grid's density, a structure's honesty) and raise the winner to match. Take the discipline, never its clothes: a borrowed button style or display face is a costume, not a raise. Write the raise down as its own line.

The standing exit: the user may always ask for the category standard played straight ("make it look like the best fintech sites"). Then convention is the commitment: ask for two or three products it should sit beside, make their craft level the bar, and execute without irony. Never steer toward it yourself.

## 5. Let the idea run the page

One thought the whole page grows from, true of this client and no other. It can live in the type, the colour, the structure, the motion, the way the product is shown, or something the visitor does. Let it decide everything from the nav to the footer: an idea that stops at the hero is a template with a good hero.

- **Unique is specific.** Put the nearest competitor's name on the page; if it still works, keep going.
- **Clean is exact, not empty.** A visible system (few type sizes far apart, one spacing rhythm, edges that line up), nothing without a job, and on every screen one thing strong enough to be its reason.
- **Full is the minimum.** A page is a whole site, not a hero and a sampler: usually six to nine sections, each built around something worth looking at.
- **Commit.** Once a direction is chosen, build it fully; a half-committed version of a good idea looks worse than either extreme. Commitment means executing the chosen look completely, not picking the strangest option.

## A vocabulary of page shapes

Name the shape you designed; never pick from this list first.

Bento grid · Long document · Marquee hero (one statement fills the first screen, then the page becomes a list, grid or prose) · Stat-led (a real figure and what it means) · Workbench (product captures as the content) · Conversational FAQ · Manifesto · Photographic (one huge image per screen) · Quote-led (a real quote) · Catalogue (variations of one thing) · Letter · Index-first (the page is a list of links) · Narrative workflow · Split studio (text one side, proof the other) · Feature stack (sticky heading pane, scroll-synced captures) · Type specimen · Portfolio grid · Map or diagram · Ecosystem index · Component playground.

## Symbols

A subject's world never licenses emblems tied to militarism, supremacy or hate movements as motifs or ornament (the Rising Sun rays, the Confederate battle flag, Nazi-era insignia and their stylised variants). Use that world's neutral forms. Content that documents such a symbol as fact stays as it is.
